Wellington puts more air pressure against a window than almost anywhere else in the country. On a strong southerly the pressure difference across a single pane is enough to push air through any gap in the seal line, which is why houses that feel fine all summer turn cold and noisy the moment the wind gets up.

The important thing to understand is that the draught is almost never coming through the glass or the frame itself. It comes through the join between the opening sash and the frame it closes against. Fixing that join is a hardware and seal job, not a glazing job.

## Working out where the air is actually getting in

The symptom tells you a lot before anyone opens anything up.

- **A cold draught you can feel with your hand** usually means the sash is not pulling in tight against its seal. That is a latch, keeper or hinge problem more often than a seal problem.
- **Rattling in gusts** means the sash has play in it. The seal has compressed or hardened and no longer takes up the gap, so the sash moves against the frame.
- **Whistling** is air being forced through a small, specific gap, often at a corner where a seal has shrunk back or at a worn keeper.
- **A draught that only appears in driving rain, along with damp**, is a different problem. That points at the mitred corners or the sill rather than the seal line, and is covered on our [draught and leak repairs](/leaks/) page.

## Why the seals fail

Aluminium joinery uses two different rubber components and people tend to mix them up. Glazing wedges hold the glass in the sash and seal that joint. Weather seals run around the sash or frame and seal the opening joint. They perish differently and they are not interchangeable.

Both harden with age and UV. Once the rubber loses its flexibility it stops springing back when the window closes, so it no longer fills the gap. In coastal suburbs the process runs faster because salt and grit work into the seal channel. Wedges that have gone hard and shiny, or that have shrunk back from the corners, are done.

The other half of the problem is compression. A seal only works if the sash is pulled in hard enough to squash it. If the latch is worn, the keeper is out of line, or the sash has dropped on tired hinges, the seal never gets compressed properly no matter how new it is. This is why replacing seals on their own sometimes changes nothing, and why we check hinge and latch condition before quoting seal work.

## What the repair involves

The work is done on site with the glass left in place. We identify whether the fault is the seal, the compression, or both, then replace wedges and weather seals with the correct profile for your joinery system, service or replace latches and handles, re-set keepers so the sash pulls in evenly along its full length, and re-align the sash or replace worn [hinges](/blog/rusted-corroded-hinges-wellington/) where it has dropped.

Getting the profile right matters. Aluminium systems from different fabricators use different seal channels, and a wedge that is close but not correct will either sit proud and stop the window closing, or sit loose and do nothing.

## Two things worth avoiding

Do not tape or foam the gap. It hides the fault, traps moisture against the aluminium, and usually damages the powder coat when it comes off.

And be aware of the trade-off. A house that was draughty was also ventilating itself, badly and expensively. Seal it up properly and you may notice more condensation on cold mornings, so keep using the window catches on their first notch, or run extractor fans, once the draughts are gone.
