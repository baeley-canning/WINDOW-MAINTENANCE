Every pane of glass in an aluminium window is held in by a strip of rubber called the glazing wedge. It gets pushed in between the glass and the frame, holds the glass tight and stops water getting past the edge. When it's new it's soft and black and you never think about it.

The sun is what wears it out. New Zealand has some of the highest UV levels in the world, and a north or west facing window in Wellington gets years of it with nothing in front of it. After enough summers the wedge on those windows is a different thing to the wedge on the shaded south side of the same house.

## What UV does to the wedge

A lot of older joinery was glazed with vinyl (PVC) wedge. Vinyl stays flexible because of the plasticisers mixed into it, and sun and heat slowly drive them out. As they go, the wedge:

- **goes hard**, so it stops pressing on the glass and just sits in the channel
- **shrinks along its length**, so it pulls back from the corners and leaves a gap
- **cracks and goes grey or chalky** on the face that gets the sun
- **works loose**, until a section sits proud of the frame or drops out altogether

Rubber wedge (EPDM) handles UV far better, but even that will harden eventually on a window that's been in full sun for decades.

## Signs your wedges are done

Walk around the house and look at the windows that get the most sun. Things to check:

- Gaps at the top corners, where the wedge has shrunk back from the mitre
- Wedge you can press with your thumb and it feels like hard plastic, not rubber
- Cracks or a grey, powdery surface
- Lengths of wedge hanging out, or missing
- Water on the inside sill after driving rain, with no obvious leak at the frame
- Glass that rattles or buzzes in a strong wind
- A whistle on windy days that wasn't there before

## Why it's worth fixing

The obvious one is water. With gaps at the corners, wind-driven rain gets in behind the glass, into the sash and down onto the sill. Over time that's what rots window reveals and stains carpet under the window. It often gets blamed on the mitres when the wedge is the problem, or both are going at once.

The second is the glass itself. Wedge that's gone hard isn't holding the pane firmly, so it can move and rattle in a northerly. That's noisy, and glass that moves in its frame is more likely to crack.

The third catches people out on double glazing. A double-glazed unit has an edge seal that keeps dry air sealed between the two panes. That seal is meant to sit covered and dry. Once the wedge shrinks back and leaves the edge open to sun and water, the seal breaks down faster, and the end result is a unit that fogs up between the panes. A new wedge costs very little. A new double-glazed unit costs a lot more.

## Leave this one to us

Silicone over the gaps is the fix most people reach for, and it doesn't last. It comes away from old wedge and can hold water in the frame, so the leak carries on out of sight.

Wedge replacement isn't a DIY job either. The wedge is what holds the glass in, so taking it out of an old window puts the pane at risk of moving, cracking or coming out of the frame.
