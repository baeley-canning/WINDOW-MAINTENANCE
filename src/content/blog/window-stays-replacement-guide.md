The stay is the folding metal arm you see when an awning or casement window is open. It does three jobs at once: it carries the weight of the sash, it controls the arc the window opens through, and it holds the window wherever you leave it. When a stay wears out you usually notice the third job failing first.

## The signs

- **The window will not hold position.** It creeps shut on its own, or the first gust slams it. The friction that held it has worn away.
- **It slams hard enough to worry you.** On an upper storey or in a wind zone this is the one to deal with quickly, because a slamming sash breaks glass and strips the fixings that hold the stay on.
- **The sash sits crooked when open**, or one end drops. One stay is worn more than the other, or an arm has bent.
- **It is stiff or gritty to open**, and getting worse. The pivots are corroding.
- **White powdery bloom on the arms**, or rust marks at the rivets. Standard in coastal Wellington and a sign the stay is near the end.
- **Visible play at the rivets.** If you can rock the arm sideways, the joint has opened up and it will not tighten again.

## Why they fail here

Salt air is the main one. In Petone, Porirua, the south coast and Kāpiti, the pivots and rivets corrode from the inside out, and a stay can look sound while the joint is already loose. Wind loading is the other. Every hard slam shocks the arm and the fixings, and repeated over a few winters that is what bends arms and opens up rivets.

The less obvious cause is a stay that was never right for the window. Stays are rated by sash weight and matched to sash width. Fit one that is under-rated, or too short in the arm, and it carries load it was never designed for and wears out early. This happens most often where a previous repair used whatever was on the shelf.

## Sizing, handing and why it matters

This is the part people are surprised by. Stays are not a single generic part. They vary by arm length, by track length, by the weight class they are rated for, and by handing, meaning a left-hand stay and a right-hand stay are different components. Double glazed sashes are considerably heavier than single glazed ones of the same size, so a stay that suited the original single glazed unit will not suit a retrofitted double glazed one.

Get any of that wrong and the window will not sit square in the frame when closed. It will not pull in evenly against the seal, which brings back the draughts and rattles the [draught-proofing](/blog/draft-proofing-wellington/) work was meant to fix. The same principle applies to [hinges](/blog/rusted-corroded-hinges-wellington/), where placement and offset change how the sash hangs.

So the tape measure comes out first. We identify the system, measure the existing stay and its mounting positions, and match a replacement on length, weight rating and handing.

## Restrictors and security stays

While the stay is being replaced it is a sensible time to think about what you want the window to do. Restrictor stays limit how far the window opens, which is worth considering for upper storey rooms where children sleep, and for ground floor windows you want to leave open for ventilation without leaving the house open. They can be fitted to most common systems and released when you need the window fully open for cleaning. More detail on our [security stays](/security/) page.

## The job itself

Stay replacement is on-site work. The glass stays in, the sash stays in the frame in most cases, and we use the existing fixing points where they are sound. We carry common patterns on the van and source the rest, including for older and discontinued joinery, and we match the finish to your existing hardware.

While we are there we check the handles, latches and hinges on the same window, because hardware that has been fighting a failing stay is usually part-worn too.
