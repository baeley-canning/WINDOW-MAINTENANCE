A ranchslider that has to be shoved is the single most common call we get. Nearly always it comes down to one of four things, and you can usually narrow it down yourself in a couple of minutes before you ring anyone.

## Narrowing it down

**Lift the door slightly by the handle as you slide it.** If it suddenly runs easily, the weight is not getting through the rollers properly. That is worn or seized rollers, or a sash that has dropped.

**Listen.** A grinding or gravelly noise is grit in the track or a bearing that has given up. A rhythmic bump every foot or so is a flat spot on a roller. Silence but heavy resistance points at alignment or a binding interlock instead.

**Look along the track at eye level.** Dents, a lipped edge, or pitting that looks like corrosion will stop even brand new rollers running smoothly.

**Check the gap down the closing edge.** If it is wider at the top than the bottom, or the other way round, the sash is out of square and it is rubbing on the frame or jamb.

**Try the lock separately** with the door already closed. If the door slides acceptably but the latch needs force, that is a keeper alignment issue and not a roller problem at all.

## What each fault actually is

**Worn rollers.** The usual culprit. The nylon tyre flattens where the door sits for months at a time, or the bearing seizes after salt and grit get into it. Coastal suburbs chew through rollers faster. Double glazed sliders are heavy, so an under-rated roller will fail early.

**Damaged track.** Dents from something dropped, pitting from corrosion, or debris packed into the channel. This one matters more than people expect, because a damaged track destroys new rollers. Fitting fresh rollers to a pitted track is why some doors go stiff again within a year.

**Dropped or out of square sash.** Most sliders have adjustment screws at the bottom of the sash that raise and lower each end. Over years of use, and with a bit of frame movement, doors settle out of square. Sometimes the adjustment has already been wound to its limit by a previous repair, which needs sorting properly rather than winding further.

**Blocked drainage.** The track has weep holes that let rainwater out. When they block, water sits in the channel, corrodes the track and the roller spindles, and the door gets progressively heavier. If you also have water tracking inside, that moves into [leak repair](/services/mitre-reseal/) territory.

## What you can safely try yourself

Vacuum the track out properly, including the corners, and wipe the channel. A surprising number of stiff doors are just packed with grit, pet hair and garden debris.

Two things not to do. Do not spray WD40 or similar in the track. It is a water dispersant, not a lubricant, it washes out what grease is left in the bearings and then attracts grit, so the door is worse in three months. And do not force a door that is dragging. Every hard shove flattens the rollers further and can dent the track, turning a straightforward roller swap into a track job as well.

## What we do on site

We assess roller condition, track damage, sash alignment and drainage together, because fixing one and ignoring the others is what leads to repeat visits. Then rollers get replaced with parts matched to your door system and weight, damaged track is refaced or capped to give the new rollers a true running surface, the sash is re-hung and squared up, keepers are re-set so the lock engages without force, and weep holes are cleared.
